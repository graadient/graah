IV
1828–1831 · The east coast of Greenland

The Glacier You Must Not Name

A king’s orders, a lost colony and one open skin boat against the entire Arctic

By the 1820s, Europe still told itself a ghost story: that the Norse settlers of medieval Greenland — silent since the 1400s — were alive behind the wall of pack ice on the island’s east coast, farming on in a hidden kingdom no ship could reach. In 1828 King Frederik VI decided to find out, and handed the mission to the family’s master mapmaker: naval captain Wilhelm August Graah. A family that had lost a castle was now sent to find a lost civilization.

Where the British hurled warships at the Arctic and watched the ice crack them open, Graah went the other way: an open skin boat rowed by Greenlandic women, kayaks scouting ahead, no lifeline home. The gauntlet was Puissortoq — “the one that shoots up” — a glacier that hurls blocks of ice the size of houses from the seabed without warning. His crew believed that saying its name out loud could wake it. When most of them refused to go on, Graah sent the scientists home and kept going with a single boat, two women from Nanortalik and one East Greenlandic family. It took three days of silence to slip past the glacier.
